To those who have grown to harbor disdain towards mathematics for any reason whatsoever, I am sorry. This writing is for you.

I aim to tackle the common misconception that mathematics is a boring, uninteresting, and unattractive field of study. I hope that after reading this, you will have a new perspective on the subject and a change of heart.

Let us begin.

Mathematics feels like the most misunderstood, misinterpreted and aridly explained subject in all of human history. For the purpose of this writing, I will ask you to abandon the notion that math is solely a science of manipulating numbers and performing operations with them. Math is first and foremost a philosophy – a way of seeing the world. It is fundamentally about imagination, creativity, flexibility, and vision.

Suppose the following, you are at a ballet and the dancer moves her body in an elegant motion, which immerses you into a captivating performance. You watch as that body twists and molds into different shapes and curves. They are continuous, they bend and cross as the dancer swings her arms front and back, and front, and back. Now take each of these imaginary curves and plot them on a Cartesian coordinate system in your mind. They are beautiful, changing, and tangible. Now watch how we can give each one meaning – a symbolic representation that we can then replicate and study over and over.

And repeat this dance forever.

Y = f(x) = x, a perfect diagonal line at 45 degrees, a stretched body with its powerful muscles.

Y = f(x) = x^2, a gentle parabola, described by the slightly curved, lifted arms of the ballerina.

Y = f(x) = x^3, an off-set motion – one arm bends downwards, while the other bends upwards.

The mesmerizing dance of mathematics.

We can interpret our surroundings in mathematical language to invent new imaginary worlds. In that sense, it resembles more what we generally call art, rather than a rigid rational science. If math can be art, we can compare it to what is often considered to be its counterpart: literature, and, for our purposes, poetry. A poet gathers inspiration from reality and redefines it to craft fictional worlds through words, spaces, and symbols. A mathematician does the same. Imagination plays an analogous role in both.

Surprisingly, math might require more imagination. Influential German mathematician David Hilbert states the following about a student who lost his aspirations for mathematics and switched to pursuing poetry: “He didn’t have the imagination \[…] to make a good mathematician – \[so] he became a poet." Perhaps the reason we fail to appreciate math is because we focus too much on the raw formulae, and we fail to see the bigger picture – the imagination and creativity that lie as if knitted into the well-known symbols. But what is the bigger picture?

For me, math feels like a serene dance woven into all the cells and rocks, and matter in the universe. Margaret Wertheim writes about this phenomenon – that math is present even within species exhibiting much different thinking capacity compared to our own. Corals, slugs, even microorganisms mold into complex geometric patterns that baffle even the most adept of mathematicians. In her essay "How to play Mathematics" Wertheim delves into meticulous mathematical pattern-chasing which suggests that math is great at describing our world. It is as if there is a cosmic blueprint that seeps into and gives structure to our entire universe and all of life and non-life in it. Why do some falcons swoop down towards their prey in a perfect logarithmic spiral? Why do electrons and planets move in elliptical patterns? Why do corals form hyperbolic crochets with their bodies? Math seems to be present all around us.

I like to imagine mathematics as an all-encompassing weave – an ether of intangible fibers of concepts that flows eternally around all parts of the universe. It is fascinating to think about. And what is even more fascinating is our human attempt to tap into this ceaseless weave and channel its power to our own goals and desires. The centuries-long struggle to make sense of this force that envelops us – it makes mathematics feel irresistible for the mind.

Mathematicians throughout history have been like tailors who have tried to push this cosmic thread through their needles and string it into forms that we could understand. Hence, the symbolic formulas we know from our school textbooks. However, the thought process behind creating these formulas is what really engages the mind of a mathematician and it is a wonder that most of us never get to experience for ourselves.

People who dislike mathematics often ask questions like: “Who invented this? Who thought of this? Who would ever care about this?” Lockhart reminds us that “mathematics, like any literature, is created by human beings for their own amusement."

Mathematics is not hard because it is incomprehensible; it is hard because it is a creative process which revolves around solving a problem. It is for the same reason that math is also not boring.

Think about how we solve problems in our daily lives. It is similar to the way we solve math problems - we generally don’t (or we find ways to cheat). We avoid matters like building strong relationships or tackling environmental issues that concern us all, because they are difficult. They are synonymous with struggle. And we do not like to struggle.

It is hard to be honest with people in your life. It is also hard to dedicate your time and energy to preserving the environment. Our response to these dilemmas is to often turn a blind eye. When we put off important conversations for as long as possible and say “someone else will do it,” we are postponing the important stuff.

This is procrastination in a nutshell. We have an innate tendency to avoid confrontation. Mathematics is misunderstood and disliked because it demands mental effort. It is this same reluctance to confront difficult matters that impedes most of us from seeing the beauty of math. Solving a good math problem is akin to confronting an important personal matter. And it is often decently challenging. But the feeling of accomplishment when you solve a good problem, is worth all the struggles.

Every single encounter with math can feel like an adventure of its own. Like any good adventure, there is something between the place we are in and the place we want to reach – the start of the problem and the desired solution. And only a challenging journey can take us there. That process of discovery, of facing the obstacles, of imagining solutions, of bending and adapting the established to craft the undiscovered. The invigorating wrestling match with life – that’s what mathematics is all about.

Math is perhaps the most honest of arts, the most humane of the humanities, and the most artful of the sciences. It is a gentle and honest study that comes from and points to the heart. In the end, learning and applying equations is not the reason why we end up loving math. We end up loving math because it helps us grow. And that should be reason enough for us to care. If you are not convinced, find a good math problem and pick up a pen and paper. You just might find more than what you came looking for. I myself am off to think about some rectangles.
