The Last Landline: Why the Payphone Outside Bell Hall Still Rings

Nobody has used it to call anyone in years. So why did three students spend a semester trying to keep it working?

By Elena Dimitrova

The phone rang at 11:40 on a Tuesday night, which is the kind of detail that sounds invented until you check the maintenance log and find it's true. Three students were sitting nearby, doing the specific kind of homework that happens on the steps outside a dorm rather than inside it, and none of them moved for a full second before someone said, out loud, "is that the payphone."

It was. It is. Outside Bell Hall, bolted to the same brick wall it's been bolted to since before most of the current student body was born, sits a coin-operated telephone that, as far as the university's facilities office can determine, has not been formally decommissioned, has not been formally maintained, and has — somehow — not stopped working.

A phone nobody asked for

Nobody on campus remembers requesting that it stay. It shows up in exactly one facilities document from 2011, listed under "miscellaneous fixtures, exterior," with no further notes. When a renovation crew widened the walkway in 2019, they built around it rather than through it, which the crew foreman later described, without much elaboration, as "just easier."

That might have been the end of it — a forgotten object slowly rusting into the landscaping — if not for a facilities ticket filed last October by a first-year named Dimitar Yanev, who noticed the dial tone while walking past at 2 a.m. and, in his words, "couldn't let it go." What followed was not exactly a restoration project and not exactly a prank. It became something closer to an informal, semester-long act of campus preservation, undertaken by three students with no facilities training and a shared conviction that the phone deserved better than being ignored.

We weren't trying to fix it. We were trying to figure out why it was still alive.

Their first discovery: the phone was never on a coin line at all. At some point in the building's electrical history, it had been quietly rewired onto the internal campus phone system — the same lines that connect security desks and RA offices — which explains both why it never went dead and why it will dial approximately four numbers on campus and nothing else.

What the phone is actually for now

These days it functions as something between a landmark and an inside joke. Students photograph it for yearbook pages. A campus tour guide has, unofficially, started pointing it out. And twice this year, according to the RA on duty, it has been used for its original purpose: someone locked out of their room, without a charged phone, dialing the one number they had memorized.

Facilities has no plans to remove it. There is, an administrator confirmed, no line item for "payphone" in next year's budget — which means, as far as anyone can tell, it will keep ringing simply because nobody has gotten around to the alternative. For a campus that runs almost entirely on group chats and shared calendars, there is something almost defiant about a piece of infrastructure that only ever does one thing, and does it whether or not anyone is paying attention.
